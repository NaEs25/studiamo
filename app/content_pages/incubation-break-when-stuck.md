H1: Stuck on a problem? Take a break and do something easy

You have been staring at the same problem for an hour: an essay that will not come together,
a design, a bug. Pushing harder rarely helps at this point. Setting the problem aside for a
while and then coming back to it often does. Psychologists call this incubation.

## What the research shows

Incubation is one of the better-studied ideas about creative thinking. A 2009 review by Ut Na
Sio and Thomas Ormerod pooled decades of experiments and found a real, if modest, benefit of
taking a break before trying again. Three details matter for how you take the break:

- It helped most on open-ended tasks, where you have to come up with many ideas, and less on
  puzzles with one right answer.
- It helped more when people had already worked on the problem for longer before the break.
- Breaks filled with demanding mental work helped less. On word puzzles, an easy task during
  the break helped more than resting.

In 2012, Benjamin Baird and colleagues tested this in one experiment with 145 students. Each
student spent two minutes per object listing unusual uses for two everyday objects, like a
brick. Then came a 12-minute break of one of three kinds, or no break at all:

- an easy task: watching digits on a screen and saying whether the occasional colored one was
  odd or even
- a demanding memory task on the same kind of screen
- sitting quietly

Afterwards, everyone listed uses again for the same two objects and for two new ones. Only the
easy-task group improved, and only on the objects they had already worked on: their ideas got
more original. Resting quietly and the demanding task did no better than no break at all.

## The part that did not hold up

Baird's team also found that the easy task gave people's minds more room to wander, and
suggested that mind-wandering was what helped. Later work has not backed that up. Across two
studies with 443 people, Murray and colleagues found no sign that mind-wandering during a
break boosts this kind of creativity. So the advice to take an easy break has better support
than the explanation of why it works.

## Walking helps too

Walking has its own evidence. In a 2014 series of experiments, Marily Oppezzo and Daniel
Schwartz asked people to think of uses for everyday objects while sitting or while walking on
a treadmill. 81 percent of them came up with more creative ideas while walking. The boost
lingered when they sat back down, and walking outside worked as well. Walking helped far less
with puzzles that have a single right answer.

## How to do it

1. Work on the problem properly first. A break helps most after real effort.
2. When you are stuck, stop for 10 to 15 minutes.
3. Do something easy that leaves your mind free: a walk on a route you know, doing the dishes,
   folding laundry.
4. Skip anything that needs real concentration, like starting another task or dense reading.
5. Come back to the same problem.

## When it will not help

A break helps you think differently. It does not help you know more. If you are stuck because
you are missing a fact, look it up. And if you keep forgetting the facts you need, that is a
job for regular review, which is what Studiamo is built for.
