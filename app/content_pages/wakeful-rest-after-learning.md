H1: Rest for ten minutes after you learn something

When you finish a lesson, the usual move is to start the next one or pick up your phone. A
small body of research suggests a better use of the next ten minutes: do nothing. Sit quietly,
close your eyes, and let the new material settle.

## What the studies found

In 2012, Michaela Dewar and colleagues read short stories aloud to 33 healthy older adults.
After each story, people recalled what they could. Then they either sat with their eyes
closed in a darkened room for ten minutes or played a spot-the-difference game for ten
minutes.

A week later, people remembered more of the story that had been followed by rest. Their
seven-day memory of the rested story was about as good as their memory of the other story
after just half an hour.

The same group later found the effect with lists of words, and with made-up words that are
almost impossible to rehearse in your head. So the benefit does not seem to come from quietly
going over the material.

## How big is the effect?

Smaller than those first studies suggest, but real. In 2019, Erin Wamsley's lab tried to
repeat one of its own earlier rest studies under a plan registered in advance, and found no
clear difference. In the same paper, the team pooled ten similar studies and found a
moderate benefit of rest for memory of words and stories.

So treat this as a cheap habit that probably helps a little, not as a memory hack.

## What counts as rest

The rest in these studies was plain: sit, eyes closed, no task. The comparison tasks, like
the spot-the-difference game, took attention but involved no words.

What clearly hurts is learning something new straight away. In a 2025 study, people who
studied extra vocabulary during the break remembered less of what they had learned before it.
Phones are less clear: in the same study, people who used social media during the break did
about as well as people who rested.

The one rule worth keeping: do not start the next lesson right away.

## Why it might work

A new memory stays fragile for a while after it forms. The leading explanation is
consolidation: the brain keeps processing new material after you stop studying, and a quiet
mind leaves it room to do that. Recordings in rats show the hippocampus replaying recent
experiences during quiet waking moments, in bursts called sharp-wave ripples. Nobody measured
that in the people in these studies, so it is the likely story, not a proven one.

## How to do it

1. Finish a study session, a lecture or a video.
2. Recall what you just learned first: answer a few questions or write down the main points.
   That was the order in the Dewar studies too: learn, recall, then rest.
3. Set a timer for ten minutes. Sit or lie down, and close your eyes or look at a blank wall.
4. Let your mind drift. You do not need to go over the material.
5. Then move on to the next thing.
