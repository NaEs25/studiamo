H1: NSDR and yoga nidra: what a 20-minute afternoon rest can do

Non-sleep deep rest, or NSDR, is a name the neuroscientist and podcaster Andrew Huberman made
popular for an old practice: lying down and following a guided body scan, much like yoga
nidra. It often comes with a striking number: a 65 percent increase in dopamine. That number
is real, but it comes from a very small study and says less than it seems to.

## Where the 65 percent comes from

In 2002, Kjaer and colleagues scanned eight experienced yoga teachers with PET twice: once
during yoga nidra, and once while they listened to speech with their eyes closed. During yoga
nidra, a tracer that competes with dopamine bound about 8 percent less in the ventral
striatum, a brain area involved in reward. The authors estimated that this matched about a 65
percent rise in dopamine release.

What to keep in mind about that result:

- It was eight people, all experienced meditators.
- It measured dopamine during the session, not afterwards.
- It does not show that NSDR refills dopamine you used up while working, or that you focus
  better afterwards. Those claims go beyond the study.

## What the larger studies show

Yoga nidra has been tested in bigger trials since, with modest results. In one online trial,
341 people listened to an 11-minute yoga nidra recording every day for 30 days. Compared with
430 people on a waiting list, they reported a little less stress, better sleep and more
well-being. The effects were very small to small. A 2025 trial over two months found
similarly small improvements, and people who practiced more regularly had lower levels of the
stress hormone cortisol across the day.

The breathing part has its own trial. In a 2023 study, people spent five minutes a day on one
of three breathing exercises or on mindfulness meditation for a month. Cyclic sighing, a deep
breath in through the nose, a second short one on top, then a long, slow breath out, improved
mood more than meditation did and lowered breathing rate. That study was about breathing, not
NSDR or dopamine.

## If you are tired, a short nap may do more

For an afternoon slump, naps have the clearest evidence. In a 2006 study, 24 young adults
slept about five hours at night, then took naps of different lengths at 3 pm. A 10-minute nap
made them more alert and sharper right away, with some benefits lasting over two hours. A
30-minute nap left them groggy at first. A 2021 meta-analysis of 11 studies found that naps
give a small boost to thinking, most of all to alertness.

## How to do it

1. Pick a time in the early or mid afternoon, when energy tends to dip.
2. Lie down or recline somewhere quiet, and set an alarm for 20 minutes.
3. Play a guided yoga nidra or NSDR recording, or scan slowly through your body from your feet
   to your head.
4. Breathe slowly, with each breath out longer than the breath in.
5. If you fall asleep, that is fine: it is a nap now. The alarm keeps it short enough to avoid
   waking up groggy.

Expect to feel calmer and more rested, not a reset of your brain chemistry.
