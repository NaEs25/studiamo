H1: Screen breaks that help: look away, step away, walk outside

After a long stretch at a screen, your eyes feel dry, your focus slips and you read the same
line three times. A break helps. Which kind, and how much, has been tested more carefully than
you might expect.

## A note on "panoramic vision"

You may have heard that softening your gaze to take in the whole room calms your nervous
system within a minute or two. The claim comes mostly from podcasts. A search of the research
turns up no peer-reviewed study showing that effect, and the studies sometimes cited for it
either could not be found or are about something else. So this page sticks to what has been
tested.

## Short breaks: more energy, less fatigue

A 2022 meta-analysis pooled 22 groups of participants, 2,335 people in all, who took
micro-breaks of ten minutes or less. People felt more energetic after the breaks and less
tired. The effect on how well they worked was small and not reliable overall. It showed up on
simpler tasks, and longer breaks helped more. After very demanding work, ten minutes may not
be enough.

## Your eyes: the 20-20-20 rule

The rule says: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20
seconds. The evidence for it is mixed.

- In a 2023 study, 29 computer users with eye-strain symptoms got reminders to follow the rule
  for two weeks. Their eye strain and dry-eye symptoms went down. A week after the reminders
  stopped, the improvement was gone. There was no comparison group.
- In another 2023 study, 30 young adults did a 40-minute reading task on a tablet with
  20-second breaks every 5, 10, 20 or 40 minutes. The breaks made no difference to their
  symptoms, reading speed or accuracy.

Looking away costs nothing, so it is still worth doing. Just do not expect much from 20
seconds alone.

## Walk outside, somewhere green if you can

In a 2008 study, 38 students did a draining attention task, then walked for about 50 minutes,
either through a park or through downtown streets. Each student did both walks, a week apart.
After the park walk, their score on a hard memory test, repeating strings of digits backwards,
improved by 1.5 digits on average. After the city walk, it improved by 0.5. The same lab
later pooled 13 experiments with 528 people and found that nature still came out ahead.

In a 2015 study, 38 people walked for 90 minutes, either through green parkland or along a
busy road. Only the nature walkers came back brooding less over their worries, with lower
activity in a brain area linked to that kind of brooding.

These walks were long. Shorter walks are less studied, but a 15 to 20 minute walk is easy to
fit into a day and gets you away from the screen.

## How to do it

- **Every 20 to 30 minutes:** look out of a window at something far away for 20 seconds.
- **About once an hour:** stand up and step away from the desk for a few minutes.
- **Once or twice a day:** walk outside for 15 to 20 minutes, through a park if there is one
  near you, with your phone in your pocket.
